Unit I: Sources & Indus Valley Sculptures

📌 1.1 Sources of Ancient Indian Iconography

A. Literary Sources

  • Vedic Texts: Rigveda mentions Indra, Agni, Varuna — no image worship, only symbolic.
  • Puranas: Vishnudharmottara Purana (Chitrasutra) — most important for Hindu iconography. Describes proportions, colors, postures.
  • Agamas & Tantras: Shaiva Agamas, Pancharatra Agamas, Shakta Tantras — rules for temple construction and idol making.
  • Buddhist Texts: Lalitavistara, Buddhacharita — describe Buddha's 32 Lakshanas.
  • Jain Texts: Acharanga Sutra, Kalpasutra — describe Tirthankaras' appearances.
  • Shilpa Shastras: Manasara, Mayamata, Samaranganasutradhara — technical manuals for sculptors.
💡 Key Point: Vishnudharmottara Purana states "One who does not know chitra (painting) cannot understand image-making rules."

B. Archaeological Sources

  • Excavated Sculptures: Mathura, Sarnath, Bharhut, Sanchi, Amravati.
  • Temple Architecture: Walls serve as galleries of iconographic development.
  • Coins: Indo-Greek, Kushana, Gupta coins depict deities.
  • Seals: Harappan seals show animals and proto-Shiva (Pashupati).
  • Inscriptions: Donative inscriptions reveal patronage and dating.

📌 1.2 Antiquity of Image Worship in India

Pre-VedicPrimitive animism — worship of natural forces
Vedic PeriodNo image worship; Yajna (sacrifice) was main form
Indus ValleySeals & terracotta figurines suggest proto-image worship
Mauryan PeriodYaksha/Yakshi cult — beginning of anthropomorphic worship
Post-MauryanImage worship firmly established in Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism
Iconography (Pratima Shastra): Science of identification, description, and interpretation of images. Includes study of symbols, attributes, postures, and proportions of divine images.

📌 1.3 Sculptures of Indus Valley Civilisation

A. Stone Sculptures

  • Priest-King (Mohenjodaro): Steatite bust, beard with trefoil pattern, shawl with trefoil motif. Height ~17.5 cm.

B. Terracotta Sculptures

  • Mother Goddess: Most common. Elaborate headdress, fan-shaped headgear, necklace. Evidence of fertility cult.

C. Metal Sculptures

  • Dancing Girl (Mohenjodaro): Bronze, c. 2500 BCE. Nude female in confident posture. Lost-wax (cire perdue) technique.
💡 Key Point: Indus artists used lost-wax (cire perdue) technique — continued for millennia in India.

Unit II: Mauryan, Sunga & Gupta Sculptures

📌 2.1 Mauryan Period (c. 322-185 BCE)

  • Polished Sandstone Pillars: Lauriya-Nandangarh, Sarnath, Allahabad. Monolithic, highly polished, animal capitals.
  • Lion Capital (Sarnath): National Emblem of India. Four lions back-to-back. Abacus with dharmachakra, bull, horse, elephant, lion.
  • Yaksha Statues: Patna Yaksha, Parkham Yaksha (~2m tall). Massive nature spirits.
  • Yakshi (Didarganj): Chauri-bearer, polished sandstone, tribhanga posture.
Mauryan Polish: Mirror-like finish on sandstone using fine abrasives. Technique lost after Mauryan period.

📌 2.2 Sunga Period (c. 185-73 BCE)

A. Bharhut (MP)

  • Stupa railing sculptures, c. 150 BCE. Red sandstone, low relief. Jataka stories. Deities with inscribed names.

B. Sanchi (MP)

  • Stupa I (Great Stupa). Four ornate Toranas. Deep relief, narrative panels. Aniconic: Buddha shown through symbols.

C. Mathura

  • Red Sikri sandstone. Buddhist and Jain images. Heavy, stiff figures with prominent ushnisha.

D. Bodh Gaya

  • Railing sculptures around Mahabodhi Temple. Lotus motifs, narrative scenes.

📌 2.3 Gupta Sculptures (c. 320-550 CE) — "Golden Age"

A. Sarnath School

  • Chunar sandstone (buff-colored).
  • Dharmachakra Pravartana Buddha: Most famous Gupta image. Seated in dharmachakra mudra.
  • Features: Transparent drapery (wet look), spiritual expression, downcast eyes, gentle smile, snail-shell curls.

B. Mathura School

  • Red sandstone tradition refined. Standing Buddha in abhaya mudra. Halos with intricate carving. More earthly and muscular.

C. Kaushambi

  • Buddhist and Brahmanical sculptures. Seated Buddha with attendants.

D. Amravati (Andhra)

  • White marble/green limestone. Sensuous, fluid style. Elongated faces, graceful postures.

Unit III: Buddhist & Jain Iconography

📌 3.1 Buddhist Iconography

A. Aniconic Representation of Buddha

  • Early Buddhism (Hinayana) avoided direct depiction.
  • Symbols: Bodhi Tree (Enlightenment), Footprints (Presence), Dharmachakra (First Sermon), Lion (Shakya Simha), Stupa (Mahaparinirvana), Lotus (Purity), Horse (Renunciation).
  • Seen at Sanchi, Bharhut, Amravati.

B. Origin of Buddha Images (c. 1st century CE)

  • Greco-Roman Theory: Gandhara school influenced by Apollo Belvedere. Wavy hair, realistic features, heavy drapery (Roman toga).
  • Indigenous Theory: Mathura school evolved from Yaksha tradition. Spiritual, ushnisha, urna, elongated earlobes.

C. Bodhisattvas

  • Avalokiteshvara (Padmapani): Compassion. Lotus, white color, swan mount. Popular in Nepal/Tibet.
  • Vajrapani: Wields vajra (thunderbolt), protector. Blue/black color.
  • Maitreya: Future Buddha. Stupa in head-dress, water-pot (kamandalu).
  • Manjushri: Wisdom. Sword (cuts ignorance) and book (Prajnaparamita). Yellow color, lion mount.
💡 Key Point: Bodhisattvas attained enlightenment but choose to remain to help others. Central to Mahayana Buddhism.

D. Female Deities

  • Tara: "Savioress." Green Tara and White Tara. Seat of compassion.
  • Prajnaparamita: "Perfection of Wisdom." Personification of text. Two arms, vajra and book.
  • Chunda: Tantric deity, multi-armed.
  • Marichi: Goddess of dawn, three faces, eight arms, riding boar.
  • Hariti: Yakshi converted by Buddha. Mother of demons turned protector of children.

📌 3.2 Jain Iconography

A. Ayagpatta (Worship Tablets)

  • Stone tablets with relief Tirthankaras. Used in Jain temples. Tirthankara seated with lion emblem below.

B. Jain Tirthankaras

  • Common features: Nude (Digambara) or white-clad (Shvetambara), padmasana or kayotsarga posture, ushnisha, srivatsa mark on chest.
  • Parsvanath: Always with seven-hooded serpent canopy.

C. Yaksha & Yakshi

  • Ambika (Kushmandi): Yakshi of Neminath. Mango tree, children, lion mount. Very popular.
  • Chakreshvari: Yakshi of Adinath. 12-armed, riding garuda.
  • Padmavati: Yakshi of Parshvanath. Snake canopy, associated with wealth.

Unit IV: Hindu Iconography (Vishnu, Shiva, Devi)

📌 4.1 Vishnu Iconography

A. Iconographical Features

  • Color: Dark blue (rain-laden cloud).
  • Four Arms: Shankha (conch), Chakra (discus), Gada (mace), Padma (lotus).
  • Garments: Yellow dhoti (pitambara). Ornaments: Kaustubha gem, Vaijayanti garland.
  • Crown: Kiritamukuta. Mount: Garuda. Consort: Lakshmi.

B. Important Forms

  • Chakravyuha: Cosmic being. Multiple forms in concentric circles. Represents universe.
  • Vaikuntha (Chaturmurti): Four-headed — human, lion, boar, demon. Different aspects of Vishnu.
  • Visvarupa: Cosmic Form. Multiple heads, arms, eyes. Arjuna sees this in Bhagavad Gita.
  • Narayana: Reclining on Shesha, Lakshmi at feet, Brahma from navel. Represents creation.
  • Harihara: Half Vishnu (right), half Shiva (left). Unity of Vaishnavism and Shaivism.
  • Dashavatara (Ten Incarnations): Matsya, Kurma, Varaha, Narasimha, Vamana, Parashurama, Rama, Krishna, Buddha, Kalki.

📌 4.2 Shaiva Iconography

A. Features

  • Third Eye: Destructive power. Matted Locks (Jata): Ganga flows from them.
  • Crescent Moon: On head. Trishula: Weapon. Damaru: Sound of creation.
  • Serpent: Around neck. Tiger Skin: Garment. Mount: Nandi (bull).

B. Important Forms

  • Anugraha: Granting boons. Varada mudra.
  • Samhara: Destroying evil. Fierce expression.
  • Uma-Maheshwar: Shiva with Parvati. Seated, Nandi below. Domestic aspect.
  • Nataraja (Lord of Dance): Dancing in ring of fire (prabhamandala).
    • Upper right: Damaru (creation) | Upper left: Fire (destruction)
    • Lower right: Abhaya mudra (protection) | Lower left: Pointing to raised foot (salvation)
    • Right foot: On demon Apasmara (ignorance) | Left foot: Raised (liberation)
  • Kalyansundar: Shiva's marriage. Brahma as priest, Vishnu holds Parvati's hand.
  • Ravananugraha: Shiva granting boon to Ravana. On Mount Kailash.
  • Ardhanarishvara: Half male (Shiva, right), half female (Parvati, left). Unity of Purusha and Prakriti.
  • Tripurantaka: Destroyer of three demon cities. Riding chariot, shooting arrow.

📌 4.3 Devi (Goddess) Iconography

  • Mahishasurmardini (Durga): 8-10 arms, riding lion, killing buffalo demon. Victory of good over evil.
  • Parvati/Uma: Consort of Shiva. Beautiful, graceful. Two arms.
  • Lakshmi: Wealth. Four arms, lotus, gold coins. Riding owl or elephant. White garments.
  • Chamunda: Fierce form. Emaciated, skull garland, seated on corpse. One of Saptamatrikas.
  • Saraswati: Knowledge, music. White color, riding swan/peacock. Veena, book, rosary, water pot.
  • Saptamatrikas (Seven Mothers): Brahmani (swan), Maheshvari (bull), Kaumari (peacock), Vaishnavi (Garuda), Varahi (boar-faced), Indrani (elephant), Chamunda (emaciated).
💡 Key Point: Saptamatrikas always shown together, flanked by Shiva (left) and Ganesha (right) or Virabhadra and Ganesha.

Shiva FormsNataraja, Ardhanarishvara, Tripurantaka
DeviMahishasurmardini, Saptamatrikas

Unit V: Pal, Pratihara, Chandella & Bronze Sculptures

📌 5.1 Pala Sculptures (8th-12th c. CE, Bengal-Bihar)

  • Material: Black basalt stone. Highly polished.
  • Features: Slender, graceful figures. Ornate halos (prabhamandala) with flame designs. Elaborate jewellery. Lotus pedestals. Intricate background carvings.
  • Subjects: Buddhist images — Tara, Buddha, Bodhisattvas most common.
  • Sites: Nalanda, Vikramshila, Kurkihar.
💡 Key Point: Pala art is the last great phase of Buddhist art in India. Many sculptures exported to Tibet, Nepal, SE Asia.

📌 5.2 Pratihara Sculptures (8th-11th c. CE)

  • Region: Gujarat, Rajasthan, Malwa, parts of UP.
  • Features: Massive, heavy figures. Ornate decoration. Large eyes, sharp features. Elaborate drapery. Temple sculptures at Khajuraho (early) and Gwalior.

📌 5.3 Chandella Sculptures (10th-12th c. CE, Khajuraho)

  • Features: Most exquisite temple sculptures. Three-dimensional, almost detached from wall. Surasundaris (celestial nymphs) — most famous. Mithuna (amorous couples) panels. Perfect proportions, tribhanga posture. Elaborate hairstyles and ornaments.
  • Temples: Kandariya Mahadeva, Lakshmana, Vishvanatha.
  • Deities: Shiva (most common), Vishnu, Devi, Surya.
💡 Key Point: Chandella sculptures represent pinnacle of medieval Indian temple art. Surasundaris show 84 poses (asanas) from texts.

📌 5.4 Bronzes — Pala, Pallava, Chola

A. Pala Bronzes (Bengal-Bihar)

  • Cire perdue technique. Buddhist deities — Tara, Buddha, Bodhisattvas. Copper alloy with silver inlay for eyes. Slender figures with elaborate prabhavali. Exported to Tibet, Nepal, Burma.

B. Pallava Bronzes (7th-9th c., Tamil Nadu)

  • Early South Indian bronze. Shiva as Somaskanda (with Uma and Skanda). Less refined than Chola. Square, heavy figures.

C. Chola Bronzes (9th-13th c., Tamil Nadu) — PEAK

  • Technique: Lost-wax perfected. Hollow casting for large images.
  • Features: Natural proportions, perfect tribhanga, expressive faces, half-closed eyes, detailed jewellery, smooth polished surface.
  • Famous: Nataraja (most famous), Devi (Parvati), Saint-poets (Nayanars/Alvars), Rama-Sita-Lakshmana-Hanuman (Utsava murtis).
  • Purpose: Utsava (processional) images for temple festivals.
💡 Key Point: Chola bronzes are considered finest in the world. Nataraja represents Shiva's cosmic dance of creation and destruction.

📌 Important Definitions
Iconography (Pratima Shastra): Science of identification, description and interpretation of images, symbols, and themes in art.
Aniconism: Practice of not showing deity in human form, using symbols instead.
Mudra: Hand gesture in Indian art conveying specific meaning.
Prabhamandala / Prabhavali: Halo or aureole behind deity's head, often decorated with flames or lotus.
Tribhanga: Triple-bend posture in Indian sculpture (head, torso, legs bent in opposite directions).
Usnisha: Cranial bump on Buddha's head representing wisdom and enlightenment.
Urna: Dot or curl between Buddha's eyebrows representing third eye of wisdom.
Lanchhana: Emblem or symbol associated with Jain Tirthankaras.
Kayotsarga: Standing meditation posture of Jain Tirthankaras.
Cire Perdue: Lost-wax bronze casting technique.

📌 Key Points — All Units
  • Vishnudharmottara Purana is the most important text for Hindu iconography.
  • Indus Valley used lost-wax technique for bronze casting.
  • Mauryan polish is unique and lost after Mauryan period.
  • Early Buddhism was aniconic — Buddha shown through symbols.
  • First Buddha images appeared in 1st century CE (Gandhara & Mathura).
  • Gupta period is called the "Golden Age" of Indian sculpture.
  • Sarnath Buddha has transparent drapery (wet look); Mathura has thick drapery.
  • All Jain Tirthankaras look identical — identified by their emblems (Lanchhana).
  • Parsvanath is always shown with seven-hooded serpent canopy.
  • Vishnu's four attributes: Shankha, Chakra, Gada, Padma.
  • Nataraja represents cosmic dance — creation, preservation, destruction.
  • Ardhanarishvara represents unity of male and female principles.
  • Saptamatrikas are seven mother goddesses always shown together.
  • Chandella art at Khajuraho shows surasundaris and mithuna couples.
  • Chola bronzes are the finest bronze sculptures in the world.
